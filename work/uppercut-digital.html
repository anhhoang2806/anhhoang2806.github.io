Uppercut Digital: thirteen Australian ad accounts, and 8.89x ROAS on the best of them

Mar 2026 – Present · Agency paid media · Paid Media Specialist — concurrent with the ELSA Speak role

I run paid media on thirteen ad accounts for eleven clients at an Australian agency, across Meta and Google Ads. This page reports what the accounts actually returned, including the one that does not work.

01 — At a glance

What does the Uppercut Digital paid media work produce?

Andrew Hoang is the Paid Media Specialist at Uppercut Digital, an Australian marketing agency, and manages thirteen ad accounts for eleven clients at the same time across Meta and Google Ads. 02 — The role

What is the job at Uppercut Digital?

Uppercut Digital is an Australian marketing agency. I am its Paid Media Specialist, and I run 13 ad accounts for 11 clients: ten Meta accounts, plus three Google Ads accounts under a single Uppercut Digital MCC.

Agency paid media is not one job repeated thirteen times. The accounts sell different things, to different people, and count different outcomes. A florist counts purchases on an occasion. A law firm counts enquiries. A kids’ sports programme counts registrations before a school holiday starts.

  • The ten Meta accounts — ten of the eleven clients, named here: Crox, Flowers on Norton St, bodytonix, EdAtlas, Waste Warriorz, Avicenna Lawyers, The Dental Practice, Smile Specialists Suite, AFL Kids, On The Nose. All Australian.
  • Channels — ten Meta ad accounts, plus three Google Ads accounts under one MCC: Avicenna Lawyers, Flowers on Norton St and Crox. Thirteen accounts in total.
  • Accounts counting purchases — Flowers on Norton St, bodytonix and On The Nose, each reporting against a purchase event
  • Accounts counting enquiries and registrations — Avicenna Lawyers reports conversions, Waste Warriorz reports leads, AFL Kids reports registrations

Waste Warriorz launched in August 2026 with 5 campaigns and has produced one lead so far. That is too early to call a result, and I am not going to call it one. It is on this page only as evidence that launching a new account is part of the job.

03 — What I found

Why do occasion campaigns beat generic monthly specials?

At Flowers on Norton St, an Australian florist, every campaign built around a calendar occasion outperformed every campaign built around a generic monthly promotion. The account returns 8.89x blended purchase ROAS across 14 Meta campaigns and 876 purchases, measured across the account’s lifetime rather than across my engagement. That blended number hides the real finding, which is the spread underneath it.

Mother’s Day 2026 returned 18.63x on 115 purchases. Valentine’s Day 2026 returned 11.68x on 83 purchases. Graduation 2026 returned 10.52x, and Mother’s Day 2025 returned 14.02x — that one ran before I joined in March 2026, so it sits inside the account’s lifetime blend without being my work. All four cleared 10x. The best generic monthly special, June Special 2026, returned 6.04x. Mother’s Day 2026 returned about three times what June Special 2026 returned, on the same account, with the same product. Between those two sits one always-on Advantage+ campaign at 8.19x, which is the single largest campaign on the account — 368 of its 876 purchases. So the ranking is not occasion versus everything. It is occasion first, always-on Advantage+ carrying the volume underneath it, and generic monthly promotion last.

The reason is not creative quality. It is that on an occasion the customer already knows they have to buy flowers and is only choosing where. A monthly special has to create the reason to buy first, and then win the choice. Two jobs instead of one.

The bottom row is a failure and it stays in the table

July Special 2026 returned 0.52x on a single purchase. It is the one outright failure in the account and it belongs in the same table as Mother’s Day, because the table is what the finding rests on. A table that only shows the winners is not evidence of anything.

What I do with the finding: occasions get the planning time and the campaign build, and the generic monthly promotion stops being the default filler between them.

04 — What I found

Does letting the algorithm prospect beat manual targeting?

At bodytonix, an Australian e-commerce account, automated prospecting beat manual prospecting by a wide margin. The account returns 6.69x blended purchase ROAS across 64 Meta campaigns, 4,703 purchases, 6,592,994 impressions and 538,573 reach.

Advantage+ Catalog prospecting returned 7.50x on 520 purchases. Manual top-funnel catalog prospecting returned 3.95x. A manual Advantage+ prospecting campaign returned 3.52x. Same account, same catalog, same period — the difference is who picks the audience.

The most efficient campaign in the account is not prospecting at all. Mid-funnel BAU retargeting returned 8.44x on 261 purchases. Bottom-funnel catalog dynamic remarketing returned 6.70x. Warm audiences convert better, which is not a surprise; what matters is that automated prospecting is the thing that keeps filling them.

The decision, and the campaign I switched off

I moved budget to the winners and switched the 3.52x manual Advantage+ prospecting campaign off. That is the whole decision. Testing that ends without a campaign being turned off is not testing, it is reporting.

Two other things in this account are worth saying out loud. Lifetime frequency is 12.24, and the mid-funnel retargeting campaign ran at 20.84 frequency — a warm audience seeing the same ads that many times is efficient right up until it is not, and it needs a creative refresh rather than more budget. And the Black Friday and discount campaigns clustered between 3.82x and 4.09x, below the account average. Discounting bought volume at a worse return than the everyday catalog work did.

05 — What I found

Can the same offer cost 83% less the second time?

AFL Kids ran the same offer twice. Holiday Camp 2026 went out as a Meta campaign in June, and again in August. Between the two runs, cost per registration fell 83%.

This is the cleanest before-and-after on the page because almost nothing else changed. Same client, same product, same platform, same conversion event. The second run is the first run with the audience, placement and creative decisions that the first run paid to learn.

The August campaign has produced 22 registrations and is still running, so the final number is not in yet. The 83% reduction is measured between the two campaigns as they stand.

06 — The other platform

What does the Google Ads work look like at Avicenna Lawyers?

Avicenna Lawyers is an Australian law firm, and it is the account where Google Ads rather than Meta carries the work. Flowers on Norton St and Crox also have Google Ads accounts, but Avicenna is the one where Search is the programme. The account is built as 4 Search campaigns segmented by practice area: Brand, Criminal Law, Commercial Law and Civil Litigation. Between 1 and 14 September 2026 it recorded 2,975 impressions and 36 conversions.

Segmenting by practice area is not a tidiness decision. Someone searching for criminal representation and someone searching for a commercial contract dispute are different people with different urgency, and putting them in one campaign means one budget and one set of bids has to serve both. Split, each practice area can be read, funded and argued for on its own.

“Limited by budget” is a recommendation, not a failure

Google flags two of the four campaigns, Criminal Law and Civil Litigation, as “Limited by budget”. That status means the campaign could serve more than it is serving. On a lead account where the practice areas are already segmented, it reads as demand the client is not currently buying — and it names exactly which two practice areas have room.

Two campaigns also carry “Missing enough relevant keywords”. That one is mine to fix, not the client’s, and it is the first thing I would work on: a capped campaign with thin keyword coverage is capped twice.

The account launched recently, so there is no long history behind these numbers. Fourteen days of data tells you what the account is doing this fortnight. It does not tell you what the account is worth, and I am not going to present it as though it does.

07 — What I would do differently

What would I do differently on On The Nose?

On The Nose is my largest account by spend and my weakest by return. It has run 24 Meta campaigns across Australia and New Zealand, produced 2,360 purchases, and returns 1.71x blended purchase ROAS.

I am not going to dress that up. Depending on margin, 1.71x is at or below breakeven for most e-commerce. The account may well be buying revenue that costs more to fulfil than it brings in, and the only person who can settle that is the client with their own margin figures in front of them. Those 2,360 purchases are real customers, and they are also why a weak ratio on an account this size matters more than a strong ratio on a small one.

The account is currently restricted because a payment failed, so the ads are not delivering at all right now. A restricted account returns nothing regardless of how well it is built, and unblocking it comes before every other item below.

I would rebuild it on what the other two accounts already proved

bodytonix says retargeting and catalog remarketing carry the return, and that automated prospecting beats manual prospecting at filling those audiences. Flowers on Norton St says discount-led and generic promotional campaigns underperform occasion-led ones, and bodytonix says the same thing in its own numbers with the Black Friday cluster sitting below the account average. On The Nose is the account where I have not yet applied either finding hard enough.

I would split Australia and New Zealand into separate campaigns

On The Nose runs across two markets and reports as one blended ratio. That means I can tell you the account returns 1.71x and I cannot tell you which market is dragging. Two markets in one campaign is one number where there should be two, and it is the reporting decision I would reverse first.

I would set a floor and act on it

I switched a 3.52x campaign off at bodytonix because it was below the account average. On The Nose has run at 1.71x across 24 campaigns without an equivalent line in the sand. The standard should not be looser on the larger account than on the smaller one.
