Uppercut Digital: thirteen Australian ad accounts in one quarter

Jul 2026 – Sep 2026 · Agency paid media · Paid Media Specialist (Freelance) — concurrent with the ELSA Speak role

From July to September 2026 I ran paid media, freelance, on thirteen ad accounts for eleven clients at an Australian agency, across Meta and Google Ads. This page separates what happened during those three months from the account history I inherited, and it includes the account that was not working.

01 — At a glance

What did the Uppercut Digital paid media work produce?

02 — The role

What was the job at Uppercut Digital?

Uppercut Digital is an Australian marketing agency. From July to September 2026 I was its freelance Paid Media Specialist, running 13 ad accounts for 11 clients: ten Meta accounts, plus three Google Ads accounts under a single Uppercut Digital MCC.

Agency paid media is not one job repeated thirteen times. The accounts sell different things, to different people, and count different outcomes. A florist counts purchases on an occasion. A law firm counts enquiries. A kids’ sports programme counts registrations before a school holiday starts.

  • The ten Meta accounts — ten of the eleven clients, named here: Crox, Flowers on Norton St, bodytonix, EdAtlas, Waste Warriorz, Avicenna Lawyers, The Dental Practice, Smile Specialists Suite, AFL Kids, On The Nose. All Australian.
  • Channels — ten Meta ad accounts, plus three Google Ads accounts under one MCC: Avicenna Lawyers, Flowers on Norton St and Crox. Thirteen accounts in total.
  • Accounts counting purchases — Flowers on Norton St, bodytonix and On The Nose, each reporting against a purchase event
  • Accounts counting enquiries and registrations — Avicenna Lawyers reports conversions, Waste Warriorz reports leads, AFL Kids reports registrations

Waste Warriorz launched in August 2026 with 5 campaigns and had produced one lead by mid-September. That was too early to call a result, and I did not call it one. It is on this page only as evidence that launching a new account was part of the job.

The reporting layer I built

Thirteen accounts cannot be read by hand every morning. I automated daily reporting across all of them: Meta data through the API, scripted extraction for sources without API access, and a database-backed pacing dashboard that keeps the previous and latest state side by side. Currency, percentage and empty fields are normalised on the way in, and anomaly rules flag an account that is over budget, has stopped delivering or is spending below 30% of its daily average. When a source fails, it is reported and skipped rather than silently left out.

03 — What the account history said

Why do occasion campaigns beat generic monthly specials?

Flowers on Norton St is an Australian florist. When I took the account over in July 2026, its campaign history already held a clear pattern: every campaign built around a calendar occasion had outperformed every campaign built around a generic monthly promotion. Those occasion campaigns ran before my engagement, so they are not my results. They are what I planned against.

In the history, Mother’s Day 2026 returned 18.63x purchase ROAS on 115 purchases, Mother’s Day 2025 14.02x, Valentine’s Day 2026 11.68x on 83 purchases and Graduation 2026 10.52x. The best generic monthly special, June Special 2026, returned 6.04x. An always-on Advantage+ campaign at 8.19x carried the volume underneath them. So the ranking is not occasion versus everything. It is occasion first, always-on Advantage+ carrying the base, and generic monthly promotion last.

The reason is not creative quality. On an occasion the customer already knows they have to buy flowers and is only choosing where. A monthly special has to create the reason to buy first, and then win the choice. Two jobs instead of one.

The two monthly specials in my window confirmed it

The two campaigns that ran during my engagement were both generic monthly specials, and both landed where the history said they would. July Special 2026 returned 0.52x on a single purchase, an outright failure. September Selected 2026 returned 5.51x, below every occasion campaign in the history. Both stay in the table, because a table that only shows the winners is not evidence of anything.

What I took from it: occasions get the planning time and the campaign build, and the generic monthly promotion stops being the default filler between them.

04 — What I did with the history

Does letting the algorithm prospect beat manual targeting?

At bodytonix, an Australian e-commerce account, the campaign history showed automated prospecting beating manual prospecting by a wide margin. These are lifetime figures for the account, most of them from before my engagement; they are the evidence I acted on, not results I produced.

Advantage+ Catalog prospecting had returned 7.50x on 520 purchases. Manual top-funnel catalog prospecting had returned 3.95x, and a manual Advantage+ prospecting campaign 3.52x. Same account, same catalog — the difference is who picks the audience. The most efficient campaign was not prospecting at all: mid-funnel BAU retargeting had returned 8.44x on 261 purchases, and bottom-funnel catalog dynamic remarketing 6.70x. Warm audiences convert better; what matters is that automated prospecting is the thing that keeps filling them.

The decision, and the campaign I switched off

I moved budget to the winners and switched the 3.52x manual Advantage+ prospecting campaign off. That is the whole decision. Testing that ends without a campaign being turned off is not testing, it is reporting.

Two other things in the history were worth saying out loud. Lifetime frequency was 12.24, and the mid-funnel retargeting campaign had run at 20.84 — a warm audience seeing the same ads that many times is efficient right up until it is not, and it needs a creative refresh rather than more budget. And the Black Friday and discount campaigns clustered between 3.82x and 4.09x, below the account average: discounting had bought volume at a worse return than the everyday catalog work.

05 — What changed in my window

Can the same offer cost 83% less the second time?

AFL Kids ran the same offer twice. Holiday Camp 2026 went out as a Meta campaign in June, before my engagement, and again in August, during it. Between the two runs, cost per registration fell 83%.

This is the cleanest before-and-after on the page because almost nothing else changed. Same client, same product, same platform, same conversion event. The August run is the June run with the audience, placement and creative decisions that the June run paid to learn.

When I read the account on 14 September, the August campaign had produced 22 registrations and was still running, so its final number is not in this record. The 83% reduction is measured between the two campaigns as they stood that day.

06 — The other platform

What did the Google Ads work look like at Avicenna Lawyers?

Avicenna Lawyers is an Australian law firm, and it was the account where Google Ads rather than Meta carried the work. Flowers on Norton St and Crox also had Google Ads accounts, but Avicenna was the one where Search was the programme. The account was built as 4 Search campaigns segmented by practice area: Brand, Criminal Law, Commercial Law and Civil Litigation. Between 1 and 14 September 2026 it recorded 2,975 impressions and 36 conversions.

Segmenting by practice area is not a tidiness decision. Someone searching for criminal representation and someone searching for a commercial contract dispute are different people with different urgency, and putting them in one campaign means one budget and one set of bids has to serve both. Split, each practice area can be read, funded and argued for on its own.

“Limited by budget” is a recommendation, not a failure

Google flagged two of the four campaigns, Criminal Law and Civil Litigation, as “Limited by budget”. That status means the campaign could serve more than it is serving. On a lead account where the practice areas are already segmented, it reads as demand the client was not buying — and it names exactly which two practice areas had room.

Two campaigns also carried “Missing enough relevant keywords”. That one was mine to fix, not the client’s: a capped campaign with thin keyword coverage is capped twice.

The account had launched recently, so there was no long history behind these numbers. Fourteen days of data tells you what an account is doing that fortnight. It does not tell you what the account is worth, and this page does not present it as though it does.

07 — What I would do differently

What would I do differently on On The Nose?

On The Nose was the largest account in the portfolio by spend and the weakest by return. Across its lifetime it had run 24 Meta campaigns across Australia and New Zealand, produced 2,360 purchases, and returned 1.71x blended purchase ROAS.

There is no dressing that up. Depending on margin, 1.71x is at or below breakeven for most e-commerce. The account may well have been buying revenue that cost more to fulfil than it brought in, and the only person who can settle that is the client with their own margin figures in front of them. Those 2,360 purchases are real customers, and they are also why a weak ratio on an account this size matters more than a strong ratio on a small one.

In mid-September the account was restricted after a payment failed, so the ads stopped delivering entirely. A restricted account returns nothing regardless of how well it is built, and unblocking it came before every other item below.

I would rebuild it on what the other two accounts already proved

bodytonix says retargeting and catalog remarketing carry the return, and that automated prospecting beats manual prospecting at filling those audiences. Flowers on Norton St says discount-led and generic promotional campaigns underperform occasion-led ones, and bodytonix says the same thing in its own numbers with the Black Friday cluster sitting below the account average. On The Nose is the account where I had not applied either finding hard enough by the time the engagement ended.

I would split Australia and New Zealand into separate campaigns

On The Nose ran across two markets and reported as one blended ratio. That means I can tell you the account returned 1.71x and I cannot tell you which market was dragging. Two markets in one campaign is one number where there should be two, and it is the reporting decision I would reverse first.

I would set a floor and act on it

I switched a 3.52x campaign off at bodytonix because it was below the account average. On The Nose had run at 1.71x across 24 campaigns without an equivalent line in the sand. The standard should not be looser on the larger account than on the smaller one.
