Craft Hub: Meta paid media across two ad accounts, 159 campaigns and 262 ad sets

June 2024 – December 2025 · Paid Media Specialist · Licensed-IP e-commerce · Meta paid media

A craft brand selling under other people’s licences, bought through two Meta ad accounts. This page reports the structure and the spread, because the account does not support a single blended return and I am not going to build one.

01 — At a glance

What is the scale of the Craft Hub Meta account?

Craft Hub is a licensed-IP e-commerce brand. It sells diamond painting kits, paint-by-numbers, 3D DIY night lamps, wooden jigsaw puzzles and 3D metal models under licences including The Lord of the Rings, Jurassic World, Wicked, How To Train Your Dragon, DC, Warner Bros and US Army. I ran Meta paid media across its two ad accounts for the full life of both, from 30 June 2022 to 30 July 2025. That is $8,522,696.82 of spend and 615,637,572 impressions, across 262 ad sets in one account and 159 campaigns in the other, and 1,218,212 link clicks, at a 2.11% all-clicks CTR and a $0.26 all-clicks CPC, in the account that reports them.

02 — The account

What was the account?

Craft Hub sells finished-in-an-evening craft kits: diamond painting, paint-by-numbers, 3D DIY night lamps, wooden jigsaw puzzles and 3D metal models. What makes it unusual as a media account is that most of the catalogue is licensed. The thing being advertised is somebody else’s intellectual property, printed onto a craft kit.

The properties I ran media against were The Lord of the Rings, Jurassic World, Wicked, How To Train Your Dragon, DC, Warner Bros and US Army. Harry Potter also ran as its own campaign. Name recognition is doing a large share of the work in the ad unit, which changes what creative testing is for: you are not testing whether the audience knows the property, you are testing which product format and which frame of the property sells it.

The work sat in two Meta ad accounts running in parallel, not one. They are structured differently and they report differently, so they are set out separately below rather than added into one row.

03 — The structure

How was the media plan structured?

Almost every buying type Meta offers is somewhere in these two accounts. Campaign budget optimisation and ad set budget optimisation both run. Advantage+ Shopping runs alongside manually built campaigns. Dynamic product ads run off the catalogue. Bidding is a mix of highest volume, manual bid cap and ROAS goal. Geography splits three ways: worldwide, US only, and explicitly no USA.

That is not indecision. Different licences, different products and different rights need different machinery, and an account that only knows one buying type has to force everything through it. The cost is that the account becomes harder to read at the top level, which is the criticism I make of it further down this page.

The repeated part is the naming: “TOP 6” appears across Wicked, LOTR, Jurassic World, Harry Potter, DC, How To Train Your Dragon and the 3D DIY lamps. That is one creative testing-and-scaling framework applied per property — six creatives in, the winners scale, the same shape every time a new licence lands. A standard structure is what makes a new property a two-day launch instead of a two-week design exercise.

The campaigns that are not in that table

Five more campaigns in the same account ran mixed objectives and report “Multiple conversions” instead of a purchase count, so they cannot appear in a table with a cost-per-purchase column. They are not small: the Advantage+ Shopping ROAS-goal Warner campaign with no USA spent $16,123.80, the US-only Advantage+ Shopping ROAS-goal campaign $12,766.43, licensed combined collections US only $11,512.82, a US-only CBO bid cap at SGD 150 $6,514.55, and the 3D DIY lamps licensed TOP 6 $4,898.60.

Leaving them out of the table and naming them here is the honest arrangement. Dropping them silently would make the visible campaigns look like the whole account, and inventing a purchase number for them would be worse.

Budgets are set per campaign, not per property

Daily budgets in the table range from $50 to $800. Wicked diamond painting runs at $800 a day and produced 1,121 purchases; DC and Harry Potter run at $50 a day. The budget is following demonstrated demand for the property rather than being spread evenly across the licence portfolio, which is the correct way round and worth stating because the opposite is a common failure in licensed catalogues.

04 — The constraint

What did licensing do to the media plan?

The most interesting object in this account is a campaign named for a rights restriction. There is an Advantage+ Shopping ROAS-goal campaign for Warner that excludes the USA, and it spent $16,123.80. Nobody excludes the largest e-commerce market in the world because they prefer the rest. That campaign exists because the licence does not cover the United States.

This is what makes licensed IP a different media job. A rights restriction is a legal boundary, and the only reliable way to hold a legal boundary inside an ad account is to make it a campaign boundary. You cannot express “we may not sell this property in this territory” as one exclusion buried in a mixed campaign, because the day someone edits that campaign for an unrelated reason, the exclusion is one checkbox away from being gone and the ad is serving somewhere it must not.

So the account carries three geographies as three sets of campaigns: worldwide, US only, and no USA. Alongside the Warner no-USA campaign sit a US-only Advantage+ Shopping ROAS-goal campaign at $12,766.43, a licensed combined collections US-only campaign at $11,512.82, and US-only CBO campaigns with bid caps at SGD 46 and SGD 150. The geography is in the campaign name, every time, so anybody auditing the account can see the boundary without opening the targeting.

What it costs to run this way

It costs learning. Splitting one property across three geographies splits its conversion signal three ways, and a campaign with a third of the conversions takes longer to leave the learning phase and optimise. It also makes cross-campaign comparison misleading: a no-USA campaign is competing for a different, generally cheaper and lower-converting set of auctions than a US-only campaign for the same product, so putting their efficiency side by side and declaring a winner would be a mistake.

That is the trade I would make again. The alternative is one campaign that is cheaper to run and one licence breach away from a problem no media efficiency saves you from.

05 — What I found

What did efficiency look like across the account?

Wide, and I am not going to smooth it. Across the named campaigns in the larger account, cost per purchase runs from $22.62 to $138.26. That is roughly a sixfold spread, on one account, in one product category. In the smaller account the ad-set numbers spread the same way: purchase ROAS observed from 0.84x to 8.84x, and cost per purchase from $8.81 to $68.07.

Four things drive that spread, and they are not equally interesting.

  • The property — Wicked diamond painting bought purchases at $28.71 and DC bought them at $57.25, on the same TOP 6 framework. Licence demand is not uniform, and no amount of media craft closes the gap between a $28.71 purchase and a $57.25 one.
  • Volume — the $138.26 figure belongs to DPA 2025 worldwide ABO, which recorded 6 purchases on $829.58. Six purchases is not a cost per purchase, it is a rounding error with a dollar sign. It sits at the expensive end of that range and it should not be read as the worst campaign, only as the smallest.
  • Geography — US-only, worldwide and no-USA campaigns are buying different auctions at different prices. Comparing them on cost per purchase alone compares markets, not media.
  • What the campaign was for — the cheapest purchases in the table, $22.62, came from a creative testing and scaling campaign. Testing campaigns are supposed to cost more, not less, so this one is a finding rather than a footnote: the testing structure was finding winners faster than the established property campaigns were exploiting them.

Craft Hub and Craft Hub.Com New · Meta Ads Manager · account lifetime

What cost per purchase cannot tell you here

Whether any of it was profitable. Cost per purchase is a cost, not a return, and a $57.25 purchase on a high-basket 3D metal model can be worth more than a $22.62 purchase on a single small kit. Revenue per order is the client’s figure and it is not mine to publish, and no revenue figure is available to me anyway, so this page reports cost and stops there.

The summary row of the smaller account shows no blended purchase ROAS at all, because attribution settings differ between its ad sets. Meta declines to blend numbers measured on different windows, which is the correct behaviour. I am not going to override it by averaging the ad-set ratios myself.

06 — What I would do differently

What would I do differently?

Meta scores the smaller account at 72 on its opportunity score and the larger one at 37. The account carrying $7,946,604.67 of the $8,522,696.82 is the one Meta rates worst. That is the single most useful line on this page and it is not a flattering one.

I would standardise the objective before adding another campaign

The results row of the larger account reads “Multiple conversions”. That is Meta saying it cannot produce one number because the 159 campaigns underneath are not counting the same thing. The consequence is concrete: there is no account-level conversion total, so there is no way to answer “how many purchases did $7,946,604.67 buy” without exporting and reconciling by hand. An account gets into that state one reasonable campaign at a time, and nobody notices until the summary row goes blank.

What I would do: fix the purchase event as the reporting objective for every campaign that sells product, and put anything measuring something else — catalogue coverage, prospecting reach, a genuine awareness test — in a separate account or under a naming prefix that makes it excludable in one filter.

I would fix the attribution window across ad sets

The same failure shows up in the other account in a different place. Its summary row has no blended purchase ROAS because attribution settings vary between ad sets, and one campaign name in the larger account still records its window in the title, “1-day click”. Attribution belongs in an account default, not in a campaign name and not in per-ad-set variation. Ratios measured on different windows are not comparable, and an account that cannot compare its own ad sets cannot rank them.

I would stop denominating bid caps in a currency the account does not report in

Two campaigns carry bid caps of SGD 44 and SGD 46 and a third SGD 150, while the account reports spend and cost per purchase in US dollars. Reading the row means holding an exchange rate in your head to know whether a $36.62 cost per purchase is anywhere near the cap that produced it. That is a small thing that taxes every review, and it is a naming and setup decision rather than a media one.

I would consolidate

159 campaigns and 262 ad sets is a lot of structure for one catalogue. Some of it is required — the rights-restricted geography splits in section 04 are not negotiable — but not all of it is, and every additional campaign divides the conversion signal a little further and makes the learning phase a little longer. The two accounts also do not share a naming scheme, which means no single filter reads across both.
